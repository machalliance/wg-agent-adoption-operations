# What we reference and cite

*A working document of the MACH Alliance Agent Adoption & Operations Working Group. September 2026 Draft, for discussion.*

These are the works that inform this framework and that it builds upon. The list does two jobs.

It tells you where we took something from somebody else. Where another group has already done the work well, we cite it and point at it instead of restating it in our own words or inventing a second vocabulary for the same idea. The [glossary](glossary.md) names the term each of these works uses for ours.

It also tells you what we will and will not cite. Standards bodies, regulators and named consortia are cited as authority. Platform vendors are cited as evidence of practice, and we say whose platform. Individual or academic work is cited only where no source in the first two groups covers the point, and we state what is behind it. A specification with one author and no adopters is not evidence that an industry has converged on anything, and citing it as though it were is how a framework launders a hobby project into apparent consensus.

It also tells you what this framework is not. Several of these works are certification standards, and this framework is not one of those. Others cover a single stage of it. If you already run a program against one of them, this page is where you find out how much of it you can keep.

## The works

- **[A2A, the Agent2Agent Protocol](https://a2a-protocol.org/latest/specification/)**: an open protocol for agent-to-agent communication. Its `AgentCard` declares an agent's identity, skills, interfaces and security schemes at a well-known address. It is the closest thing to a per-agent document that has real adoption, and the platform agent registries follow its shape. It carries no field for the accountable person, the risk classification, the sign-off, the sensitivity of the data reached, or the escalation path. So it does not replace our stage 1. It complements it, and where the two overlap you should generate one from the other. A partner to stages 1 and 3.
- **[AIUC-1](https://aiuc-1.com/)**: an agent standard you can certify against, from the AIUC-1 Consortium. It covers data and privacy, security, safety, reliability, accountability and society. It adds third-party audit. It publishes maps to the NIST AI RMF, ISO 42001, the EU AI Act, MITRE ATLAS and OWASP. It touches all five of our stages, as criteria to certify against. We are different: we say how to operate, not what to certify. Our stage 5 cites its maps rather than deriving new ones.
- **[Anthropic, Our framework for developing safe and trustworthy agents](https://www.anthropic.com/news/our-framework-for-developing-safe-and-trustworthy-agents)**: five commitments from a model provider: keeping humans in control, transparency in agent behavior, aligning agents with human values, protecting privacy across extended interactions, and securing agents' interactions. It states principles rather than controls. It matches our stages 1 and 4. The first commitment is where our stage 1 sections on what an agent decides alone, and on escalation, come from.
- **[ASG-WG](https://www.asg-wg.org/)** and the **[WEF AI Governance Alliance](https://www.deepinspect.ai/blog/ai-governance-alliance)**: other multi-party groups that work at the same level as this working group.
- **[AWS Well-Architected Agentic AI Lens](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/agentic-ai-lens/agentic-ai-lens.html)**: architectural guidance for agents on AWS, organized as best practices under the six Well-Architected pillars. It is the most detailed control set on this page. It is also vendor-specific by design. It matches our stages 1, 3 and 4. AGENTOPS01 asks for agent roles, success criteria and handoff procedures, which is our stage 1 outcome. AGENTSEC03 and AGENTREL02 are our stage 3 least privilege. AGENTOPS05 and AGENTOPS06 are our stage 4 monitoring and evals.
- **[EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/)**, in particular **[Article 11](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/11/)** and **[Annex IV](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/4/)**: law, and not a framework you choose. Annex IV sets out, in nine sections, the technical documentation a provider of a high-risk system must draw up before that system reaches the market. Section 1(a) is an intended purpose. Sections 2(e) and 3 are the human oversight measures required under Article 14. Section 9 is a post-market monitoring plan. Those match our stages 1, 4 and 5. We do not print the compliance dates. They have moved more than once, most recently by the Digital Omnibus on AI, and the Annex III deadline moved separately from the Article 50 transparency duties. Check them at the source. If an agent of yours is in scope, this is the only work on this page that carries a fine.
- **[Cloud Security Alliance, Agentic AI Identity and Access Management](https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/artifacts/agentic-ai-identity-and-access-management-a-new-approach)**: a purpose-built approach to agent identity, published August 2025. It covers agent identity, delegation you can trace back to a person, attribute-based authorization, revocation across systems that do not share a login, and credentials that live only for one task. It is free to download. Our stage 3 Platform Control Levels are a plain-language on-ramp to it, and name it as the place to go when three levels stop being enough.
- **[FIDO Alliance, Agentic Authentication Technical Working Group](https://fidoalliance.org/fido-alliance-to-develop-standards-for-trusted-ai-agent-interactions/)**: standards for agent identity, delegation and authorization across vendors. A partner to one part of stage 3, the agent login, and to nothing else in it.
- **[Gartner AI TRiSM](https://www.gartner.com/en/articles/ai-governance-trism)**: Trust, Risk, and Security Management. It covers AI governance, runtime inspection and enforcement, information governance, and infrastructure. It is the closest match to stages 2, 3 and 4.
- **[Gartner Market Guide for Guardian Agents](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-04-28-gartner-identifies-six-steps-to-manage-artificial-intelligence-agent-sprawl)**: a new group of products that supervise other agents. They cover visibility, continuous assurance, and runtime enforcement. A match to stage 4.
- **[Google, An Introduction to Google's Approach for Secure AI Agents](https://research.google/pubs/an-introduction-to-googles-approach-for-secure-ai-agents/)** and the **[Secure AI Framework](https://safety.google/intl/en/safety/saif/)**: three principles, stated more compactly than anywhere else we have found. Agents must have well-defined human controllers, their powers must be carefully limited, and their actions and planning must be observable. Those map to our stages 1, 3 and 4 in that order. SAIF 2.0 adds an agent risk map. Read this first if you want the shortest statement of what agent governance is for.
- **ISO/IEC 42001**: the AI management standard that you can certify against. It is the natural partner to stage 5.
- **[Microsoft Entra Agent ID](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/id-governance/agent-id-governance-overview)**, with **[agent identity in Microsoft Foundry](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/agents/concepts/agent-identity)**: an identity type for agents in an enterprise directory. An agent can then be inventoried, given least privilege, and audited with the same tooling as a person or a service. A partner to our stage 3 agent login. It is also the clearest sign that the agent login is becoming a platform primitive rather than something you assemble.
- **[NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)**, with the **[Cloud Security Alliance Agentic AI Profile](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/agentic/agentic-nist-ai-rmf-profile-v1/)**: four functions (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) and extra categories for agents. GV.1.6 requires an inventory of AI systems resourced according to risk priority, which is what our stage 1 documents amount to. Its runtime records and autonomy checks match how stage 4 feeds back into stage 2. Its four autonomy levels belong in stage 3, beside the platform control level, and not in the stage 2 classifications: autonomy measures how much an agent does without a person, and a classification measures how bad it is when the agent is wrong.
- **[OpenTelemetry semantic conventions for generative AI](https://opentelemetry.io/blog/2026/genai-observability/)**: vendor-neutral conventions for what an agent emits, from the CNCF project most observability tooling already speaks. They define the span names, attributes, metrics and events for model calls, tool executions, agent runs, retrieval and memory, so a trace means the same thing across frameworks and vendors. The agent spans are still marked experimental, so read their current status before you build against them. A partner to stage 4: our Monitoring Levels say how much of a run to keep, and these say what shape to keep it in.
- **[OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications](https://genai.owasp.org/resource/owasp-top-10-for-agentic-applications-for-2026/)** and **[State of Agentic AI Security and Governance v2.01](https://genai.owasp.org/resource/state-of-agentic-ai-security-and-governance/)**: ten ranked risks for agentic systems, from goal hijack and tool misuse through to rogue agents. Write your stage 4 incident playbook against this list. These are the ways agents fail, and generic software incident response does not cover them. The governance report adds a maturity model. It pairs what an organization runs with its capability to oversee it, which is the same two-axis idea as our platform control levels and monitoring levels. It matches stages 2, 4 and 5.
